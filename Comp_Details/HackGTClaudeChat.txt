  1. Why the billing idea's main user is a biller

  Drugs given in the clinic, like cancer infusions and injections, use a model
  called buy-and-bill:
  1. The clinic buys the drug up front, often thousands of dollars per dose.
  2. A nurse gives it to the patient.
  3. The clinic bills insurance and hopes to get paid back.

  The doctor decides to prescribe. But the codes, units, NDC formatting,
  modifiers and invoice attachments are handled by billing staff, often a
  separate person or an outsourced company. Doctors never touch a J-code. Every
  screen in your current doc (claim templates, NDC formats, wastage modifiers)
  is biller work.

  In pharma language, "HCP" usually means prescribers (physicians, nurse
  practitioners, physician assistants), which is who Impiricus targets. Billers
  aren't HCPs, so a biller-centered demo is weak on "Impact on the HCP."

  It can be HCP-centered. The doctor has real pain here:
  - The decision: doctors often avoid new drugs because the practice lost money
    on one before. The physician owns that risk decision.
  - Documentation: many denials come from missing medical-necessity
    documentation. Writing the chart note is the doctor's job, so "here's
    exactly what your note must say for this drug" is doctor value.
  - Patient eligibility: does this patient match the drug's label (indication,
    weight-based dose, contraindications)?

  So the doctor starts the flow, and the biller, nurse and front desk get their
  tasks as a result. Then the doctor is the protagonist and the staff are
  supporting cast.

  2. What CoverMyMeds does

  CoverMyMeds handles electronic prior authorization. When insurance requires
  approval before covering a prescription, it replaces faxes and phone calls
  with an online form. It's free for doctors and pharmacies, and pharma
  companies pay for it, because a stalled prior authorization means an abandoned
  prescription and a lost sale. McKesson bought it for about $1.1B.

  It matters to you for two reasons:
  - It proves the business model: pharma pays for tools that remove access
    friction for HCPs. That's your billing idea's model.
  - It shows prior auth is taken. CoverMyMeds owns it, and two teams already
    built it here last year. Don't let your billing idea drift into prior auth.

  3. Multiple codes: yes, and you should

  One administered dose actually involves several code types:

  Code type: Drug code (HCPCS)
  Examples: J3490 (unclassified drugs), J3590 (unclassified biologics), J9999
  (unclassified cancer drugs), later a permanent J-code
  What it covers: The drug itself
  ────────────────────────────────────────
  Code type: Administration code (CPT)
  Examples: 96413 (chemo infusion, first hour), 96365 (non-chemo IV infusion),
  96372 (injection)
  What it covers: The act of giving it. Start and stop times decide which one
  applies, which is why that documentation matters.
  ────────────────────────────────────────
  Code type: Modifiers
  Examples: JW (drug wasted), JZ (no waste)
  What it covers: Leftover drug from single-dose vials
  ────────────────────────────────────────
  Code type: Diagnosis code (ICD-10)
  Examples: Varies
  What it covers: Has to match the drug's approved indication

  A feature idea: when CMS assigns the permanent J-code (codes are updated
  quarterly), your tool switches every template over automatically. Something
  like "The day your code changes, your claims change."

  Scope it down: Medicare rules only (they're public; commercial payers vary),
  and the physician office only (hospital outpatient uses different codes).

  4. Is TrialMatch too simple?

  As written, yes, and Trialify is the proof. "Upload report, get top 3 trials"
  is a feature, not a product. What's missing is the rest of the real workflow.
  Here's what's actually hard about trials for a community oncologist:

  - "Almost eligible" is the useful answer. Most patients don't cleanly qualify.
    Mark each criterion met, not met, or unknown, with evidence quoted from the
    notes. Turn the unknowns into actions, like "Order a KRAS test and 3 more
    trials open up."
  - Timing: many trials exclude patients who had certain treatments in the last
    N weeks, so the choice made today can close off options. Showing an
    eligibility deadline is a real, non-obvious feature.
  - Logistics: the distance to the nearest site that's actually recruiting, and
    the travel burden. ClinicalTrials.gov has status for each site.
  - Proactive instead of reactive: scan the doctor's whole patient list and flag
    "2 of your patients newly qualify for a trial that opened this week."
    Patients who don't qualify now stay on a watch list. This is the trigger
    model that fits Impiricus. Caveat: it's close to Vital's winning concept, so
    judges may compare the two.
  - Fear of losing the patient: community doctors worry that referring a patient
    to an academic center means losing them. A referral packet that sets up
    co-management answers a real emotional objection.
  - Proof of accuracy: the TREC Clinical Trials benchmark has patient
    descriptions paired with expert trial relevance labels. Saying "we measured
    our matcher at X% on TREC" beats every trials project last year on technical
    execution.

  5. Would a two-sided TrialMatch drift from the prompt?

  Not if you frame it right. The prompt is "HCP engagement," and Impiricus's
  customers are pharma companies that want to engage HCPs, so a sponsor side is
  literally Impiricus's business. Medicus was two-sided and won 1st. But Luma
  was also two-sided and didn't place, so two-sided isn't magic by itself.

  Rules to stay aligned:
  - About 70% of the demo is the doctor side. The sponsor side is one screen
    explaining why pharma would pay.
  - Frame the sponsor side around HCPs: which regions have doctors seeing
    eligible patients, where to open sites, and live slot availability. Slot
    availability isn't public on ClinicalTrials.gov, which gives the sponsor
    side a real reason to exist.
  - Avoid pure protocol-design analytics, like "which criteria block
    enrollment." That drifts toward R&D and away from engagement.
  - Privacy: sponsors only ever see aggregated, de-identified data, and the
    doctor chooses to send a referral. Judges will ask.

  6. The billing idea, fully fleshed out

  MVP features:
  1. Doctor's "Start therapy" flow:
     - Check the patient against the real FDA label from openFDA, with
       citations.
     - List the documentation the note must include.
     - Estimate reimbursement against drug cost. Medicare's ASP pricing file is
       public, and Medicare pays roughly ASP + 6%.
     - Give a clear "financially safe to proceed" signal.
  2. Role board with live updates: the nurse records start/stop times and waste,
     and the biller's claim fills itself in.
  3. Claim builder in plain code, not LLM: code selection, units, 11-digit NDC,
     JW/JZ modifier and the description line that unclassified codes require.
  4. Pre-submission checker that flags problems before the claim goes out.
  5. Escalation to the drug maker's field reimbursement manager (FRM), with the
     problem already packaged.
  6. Permanent-code switchover (from section 3).
  7. Optional sponsor view: which clinics are stuck and on what, so the drug
     maker knows where to send its FRMs.

  Questions you haven't asked yet:
  - Demo drug: use a real, recently approved, clinic-administered drug that's
    still on an unclassified code. Check it in CMS's quarterly HCPCS updates,
    since a real drug makes the demo credible.
  - Emotional hook: the current doc is dry. Tell a patient story: a community
    clinic can't risk a $30k dose, so the patient drives 2 hours to a hospital
    or never gets the drug.
  - Anti-kickback law: judges may ask whether free, pharma-sponsored billing
    help is an illegal inducement. Drug makers' patient-support programs do this
    legally when help stays specific to their product. Keep it that way and
    have the answer ready.
  - Does Impiricus already offer reimbursement support? Ask at the booth.

● The generic codes

  Every drug given in a clinic is billed with a HCPCS code, a billing code set
  run by CMS. Established drugs have their own permanent code. For example,
  J9271 is Keytruda, billed per 1 mg. A newly approved drug doesn't get its own
  code until CMS processes an application, which it reviews quarterly. That
  usually takes months.

  Until then, clinics use a catch-all "not otherwise classified" (NOC) code:

  ┌───────┬─────────────────────────┬───────────────────────────────────────┐
  │ Code  │         Meaning         │               Used for                │
  ├───────┼─────────────────────────┼───────────────────────────────────────┤
  │ J3490 │ Unclassified drugs      │ Most regular (small-molecule) drugs   │
  ├───────┼─────────────────────────┼───────────────────────────────────────┤
  │ J3590 │ Unclassified biologics  │ Antibodies, proteins and other        │
  │       │                         │ biologics                             │
  ├───────┼─────────────────────────┼───────────────────────────────────────┤
  │       │ Not otherwise           │                                       │
  │ J9999 │ classified,             │ Cancer drugs                          │
  │       │ antineoplastic          │                                       │
  ├───────┼─────────────────────────┼───────────────────────────────────────┤
  │       │ Unclassified drugs or   │ The same idea, but for hospital       │
  │ C9399 │ biologicals             │ outpatient departments (skip this for │
  │       │                         │  the demo)                            │
  └───────┴─────────────────────────┴───────────────────────────────────────┘

  The real problem is that these codes say nothing about which drug was given.
  Hundreds of different drugs share J3490. So the payer can't process the claim
  automatically. A person has to read the extra details, figure out what the
  drug is, and decide how much to pay, because there's no set price. Every
  missing detail means a denial or a delay.

  The current process and the documents involved

  1. Before treatment
  - Benefits check / prior authorization: does the patient's insurance cover
    this drug? This is often done through the drug maker's support program
    ("hub").
  - Purchase invoice: the clinic buys the drug. The invoice shows the drug name,
    NDC (the drug's product ID), quantity and the price the clinic paid. Keep
    it, because payers often ask for it.

  2. Day of treatment
  - Physician order: drug, dose (often by weight, e.g. mg/kg), route and
    frequency.
  - Doctor's progress note: the diagnosis and the reason for the drug. It must
    match what the drug is FDA-approved for, including required test results
    (e.g. a biomarker) and prior treatments. This is the "medical necessity"
    documentation.
  - Nurse's infusion record:
    - Drug, NDC and lot number.
    - Exact dose given and amount wasted. Leftover drug in a single-use vial
      gets thrown away and must be documented.
    - Start and stop times. These decide the administration code: an infusion
      under about 16 minutes counts as a "push," and longer infusions are billed
      by the hour.

  3. The biller builds the claim. For a physician office this is the CMS-1500
  form, or its electronic version, the 837P. For a miscellaneous code it needs:
  - Drug line:
    - The code (J9999, etc.) plus a waste modifier: JW for the line billing the
      discarded amount, or JZ meaning nothing was wasted. Medicare requires one
      of them.
    - Units. For miscellaneous codes, Medicare contractors typically want 1
      unit, with the real dose written in the description. Other payers differ,
      which is one reason errors happen.
  - NDC section: the NDC converted to an 11-digit format (labels often print 10
    digits in different layouts), plus a unit type and quantity.
  - Description field (box 19 / line note): drug name, strength, dose given and
    route, e.g. "DrugX 500 mg IV." Without it, the payer doesn't know what the
    drug was.
  - Diagnosis codes (ICD-10) that match the FDA-approved use.
  - A separate administration line with its own CPT code (e.g. 96413, chemo
    infusion, first hour).
  - Attachments when requested: the invoice, the drug's FDA label and the
    medical notes.

  4. Payer review. A person reviews the claim manually and sets the payment from
  the invoice or list price. If anything is missing, they send a request for
  records or deny it. This takes weeks, while the clinic is already out
  thousands of dollars.

  5. The permanent code arrives. On its effective date, everything changes: new
  code, and billing units based on the code's definition. For example, a code
  "per 1 mg" turns a 500 mg dose into 500 units. A miscellaneous-code claim
  after that date gets denied.

  A correction to your billing doc: its "500 mg → 500 units" example applies
  once the permanent code exists. It isn't the usual Medicare rule during the
  miscellaneous-code period. Double-check this with a Medicare contractor's
  guidance page before pitching, since exact rules vary by payer.

  How our AI automates it

  - Reads the FDA label from openFDA. It picks the right generic code (cancer
    drug → J9999, biologic → J3590, otherwise J3490) and pulls out the drug
    name, strengths, vial sizes, NDCs, approved uses and storage requirements.
  - Reads the doctor's note and the nurse's record (mock patient records for the
    demo). It pulls out the dose, waste, times and diagnosis. It also flags
    what's missing before submission, like a missing stop time or a diagnosis
    that doesn't match an approved use.
  - Fills in the claim with regular code, not AI: NDC conversion, units, JW/JZ
    modifier, the description line and the administration code based on the
    infusion times.
  - Assembles the full packet (claim, invoice, label, notes), so nothing gets
    sent back.
  - Watches CMS's quarterly code updates and switches everything over when the
    permanent code goes live.

  What details a generic-code claim needs

  This is from Medicare contractors' official billing guidance:

  Detail: Drug name
  Where it goes: Item 19 of the CMS-1500 claim form (a free-text box, 80
  characters max)
  Why: The code (e.g. J9999) doesn't say what the drug is
  ────────────────────────────────────────
  Detail: Strength and dose given (e.g. 500 mg)
  Where it goes: Item 19
  Why: So the payer knows how much to pay for
  ────────────────────────────────────────
  Detail: Route (IV, injection, etc.)
  Where it goes: Item 19
  Why: Part of describing the drug
  ────────────────────────────────────────
  Detail: NDC (the drug's 11-digit product ID)
  Where it goes: NDC section of the claim line
  Why: Identifies the exact product and package
  ────────────────────────────────────────
  Detail: Invoice cost
  Where it goes: Item 19 or an attachment (some contractors)
  Why: There's no set price, so the payer uses it to decide how much to pay
  ────────────────────────────────────────
  Detail: Units = 1
  Where it goes: Units field
  Why: Medicare's rule for generic codes: always 1, no matter the dose. That's
  the opposite of the permanent-code rule, which trips billers up.

  In Noridian's words (Noridian is a Medicare contractor): "If required
  information is missing, the code will be deemed unprocessable." The claim gets
  bounced back unpaid and has to be fixed and resubmitted.

  Are the details hard to give, and do claims get denied?

  None of the details is hard on its own. The problem is that each claim is
  manual, error-prone and inconsistent:
  - Someone has to hand-type a description into an 80-character box, correctly,
    on every claim.
  - Rules differ by payer. Medicare wants units = 1; commercial payers can want
    something different.
  - NDCs are printed in several 10-digit layouts and must be converted to the
    11-digit format.
  - No computer can price the claim, so a person reviews it. Billing industry
    sources put this at 45–90 days with commercial payers, but those are
    billing-company blogs, not studies.
  - The rules flip mid-stream. When the permanent code arrives, a claim that
    still uses the generic code is automatically denied.

  The timeline

  Example: FDA approves "DrugX," an infused cancer drug, on February 10.

  The drug's timeline (how long the generic-code period lasts)
  - Feb 10: FDA approval. Clinics can buy and give the drug immediately, but can
    only bill it as J9999.
  - Early April: The drug maker applies to CMS for a permanent code.
    Applications are due on the first business day of each quarter and require
    FDA approval first, so they missed January.
  - April to October: CMS reviews the application.
  - Oct 1: The permanent code (e.g. "J9xxx, per 1 mg") takes effect.
  - Result: about 7.5 months on the generic code. Depending on when approval
    lands relative to a deadline, it's usually about 6 to 9 months, and longer
    if CMS doesn't approve the first application.

  One patient's timeline (repeats for every patient during those months)
  1. The doctor decides the patient should get DrugX and writes the order and a
     note explaining why (diagnosis, test results, prior treatments).
  2. Insurance check: staff confirm the patient's plan covers it. Commercial
     plans may require prior authorization, which takes days to weeks.
  3. The clinic buys DrugX from the distributor, say about $15,000, and keeps
     the invoice.
  4. Treatment day: the nurse records the dose given, the amount wasted, and
     start and stop times.
  5. The biller builds the claim:
     - Drug line: J9999, units = 1, plus the waste modifier (JW or JZ).
     - Item 19: "DrugX 500mg IV" plus the NDC and invoice cost.
     - Diagnosis code and a separate line for the infusion itself.
     - Invoice and notes attached if the payer asks.
  6. The payer reviews it by hand. If anything is missing, the claim is returned
     as unprocessable, or the payer asks for records. The biller fixes it and
     resubmits, which adds weeks.
  7. Payment, often 1.5 to 3 months after treatment. Meanwhile the clinic has
     already bought doses for the next patients, so it may be carrying tens of
     thousands of dollars.
  8. Oct 1, the code switches: every claim for treatment from Oct 1 onward must
     use the new code, with units = dose ÷ the code's unit (500 mg on a "per 1
     mg" code = 500 units). A missed switch means an automatic denial.

  The result: a doctor's first experience with a new drug is waiting on money
  and fighting paperwork. Many practices wait for the permanent code, so the
  drug's first 6 to 9 months are slower than they should be. That's the gap your
  product fills.

  Where the AI fits (one line for your notes): it reads the FDA label and the
  patient's chart, fills in Item 19, the NDC and units for that payer, catches
  missing details before submission, and switches everything when the permanent
  code goes live.

  Can't they just do it themselves?

  They can. Some real alternatives already exist:
  - Drug makers publish billing guide PDFs for each launch, with a sample
    filled-in claim form.
  - Drug makers employ reimbursement specialists (FRMs) who visit clinics.
  - Claim-checking software (Waystar, Availity) catches general formatting
    errors.

  What those don't do:
  - The billing guide is a static PDF. It doesn't check this patient's note,
    dose and waste.
  - The reimbursement specialists are human and don't scale to every clinic.
  - Claim-checking software doesn't know anything about new drugs.

  The honest weak spot: once a biller has done 3 claims for DrugX, they can copy
  the pattern. So the value is concentrated in:
  - The first claims for each new drug. Dozens of new drugs are approved each
    year, so a clinic keeps facing this.
  - Checks specific to each patient, especially #8, the doctor's documentation.
  - The code switchover (#5 and #11).

  That lines up with what pharma cares about most: the launch window. But be
  ready for a judge to ask "Wouldn't they just learn it?" The answer is "Yes,
  for one drug. Not for every new drug, every payer and every code change."
